Behind a high walled frontage in St Ives sits a house facing the three big questions of any serious renovator's dream: do you demolish, do you renovate, or do you quietly ring a production company and offer the place up as a set for something post-apocalyptic? Because that's genuinely the vibe. Palms and vines have worked the brick facade over so thoroughly that the handsome red double front doors now read less like an entrance and more like a sealed bunker hatch, the kind a nervous survivor prises open in act one. It is, allegedly, still a family home. Whether it currently agrees with that assessment is a separate question.

Inside, it's a full, undisturbed time capsule, dust included: a fully mirrored stairwell in the entry, a formal lounge in dusty pink with an ornate ceiling rose and a crystal chandelier, and an upright piano that has clearly been retired since well before this decade. The kitchen commits hard to 1980s leadlight cabinetry and an aqua tile splashback, and out back there's a rumpus room with raked timber ceilings, a proper brick bar, and a pool table under a sheet, on indefinite standby. All of it beautifully, eerily intact. None of it something you'd want to lean on too hard before a building inspector's had a look.

And then there's the pool, which has stopped being a pool. Is it a pool or a pond at this point? A trick question — it's neither, it's an ecosystem. Fully, aggressively green, edge to edge, doing more active photosynthesis than most of the garden around it. Add the jungle canopy pressing in from every side and you've got a backyard that's less 'entertaining area' and more 'site requiring a permit and possibly a guide.'

On the numbers: a 929.5sqm level block, five bedrooms across two levels, drive-through garage access, and a location an easy walk to some of Sydney's better-known private schools, with Gordon station and the Northern Beaches both within reach. The agents are calling it a rebuild, renovate or brand-new home opportunity, which is doing a lot of quiet, diplomatic lifting. Realistically, this isn't a weekend reno — it's a full structural assessment followed by a very honest conversation with your builder about what's actually salvageable. But the block is good, the bones might be too, and somewhere under all that green, there's still a house in there wanting to find out.
