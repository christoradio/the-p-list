VENUE REVIEW: 9 ROBSON ROAD, KENTHURST. Sold, but some venues demand a write-up regardless. Real Studio 54, for the uninitiated, ran in Manhattan from 1977 to 1980 — seventeen hundred people a night, a clientele that ran from Bianca Jagger to Andy Warhol, and an ending delivered not by fire code or noise complaint but by a very thorough tax evasion charge against its owners. This Kenthurst version has none of that scandal on the books yet. Give it time.

**The room.** Nine Robson Road sits on 5 arable acres with dual road frontage, about 45 minutes out of Sydney in the Hills District's acreage belt — the kind of pocket built for equestrian properties and large families, not, historically, nightclubs. This one does not care. Seven double bedrooms, full brick, entered through a round portico held up by columns you could genuinely hug, under a Juliet-style balcony wrapping the entire upper level. The driveway alone is wide enough to function as an actual car park, which, as it turns out, is exactly the point.

**The décor.** Twin curving staircases descend under not one but three crystal chandeliers hung low enough to graze a tall guest's head on the way to the bar, the whole run carpeted wall-to-wall in crimson. Some windows are red. Some walls are red. There is, at a rough count, more red carpet in this house than in most actual cinemas. Score: five stars, zero restraint.

**The main room.** Past the grand stairs, the venue proper: a sprung dance floor, mirror ball, exposed brick, and its own red-carpeted stairwell up to a private bar. Next door, because apparently a disco alone doesn't cover the entertaining, a fully fitted recording studio with a soundproofed booth — for the nights that need a live album to remember them by.

**The amenities.** Bathrooms here read like they were decorated by committee, and possibly a committee that had already had a big night downstairs. One goes bronze mirror tile with a floral motif and a sunken spa; the other commits fully to crimson, with a red hexagonal jacuzzi that looks poached from a wedding cake. The kitchen, meanwhile, is doing its most sincere 1988 impression — cream cabinetry, biscuit tile — and there's a second one downstairs for when the main one gets busy. Further down the floor plan: a squash court, a full tennis court with its own pavilion, a cricket practice wicket, and an entertaining deck measuring a genuinely absurd 15 metres by 15, easily large enough to double as a go-kart track on a slow weekend.

**The crowd.** Unconfirmed, but judging by the setup — three chandeliers, a live recording booth, a spa built for two — we're guessing this was never a quiet-night-in kind of house.

**The verdict.** It last sold under the description "iconic property," which, unusually for real estate copy, actually tracks. It's since changed hands, so somewhere out past that Blue Mountains view there's a new owner deciding whether the disco stays, whether the recording studio gets first dibs on a reno, or whether the whole thing gets preserved exactly as is, out of sheer respect. Whoever grew up filming a 21st down that red staircase has lived a childhood the rest of us can only aspire to. How decadent can one house get? Turns out: exactly this decadent. Four stars. Would attend again.
