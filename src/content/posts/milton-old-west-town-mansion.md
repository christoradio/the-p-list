Look, we've seen big houses. We've seen tacky houses. We've seen houses with home casinos and houses with life-size Jesus statues and houses that appear to have been decorated entirely out of a floristry warehouse's returns bin. None of them prepared us for a 24,000-square-foot neoclassical estate on nine-plus acres in Milton, Georgia that, behind a completely unassuming interior door, is concealing an entire simulated 1880s frontier town, because someone with a 125KVA emergency generator and 22 individually zoned HVAC systems looked around one day and thought: still not quite Tombstone enough in here.

The "normal" 19,600 square feet of the house — and we use "normal" the way you'd use it about a friend who owns three Ferraris and calls it a starter collection — plays it almost convincingly straight. Three domed ceilings, one of them 27 feet over the living room, because a mere 15-foot ceiling apparently reads as pokey. Floor-to-ceiling glass everywhere a load-bearing wall would otherwise have had the audacity to exist. Seven different marble patterns across the floors, 153 feet of groin-vaulted hallway for absolutely no reason anyone could name under oath, and mahogany cabinetry so relentless it colonises the kitchen, the office, the library and the primary bedroom like it's trying to make a point. It is, genuinely, a serious and expensive piece of architecture. Right up until the door marked "Saloon."

Because the saloon is not a bit. It is a full, working, fully licensed-looking bar with stained glass, a velvet-curtained stage, café tables and enough theatrical detailing to make you check twice for a gift shop. Wander further down the street and there's a boarding house, a general store, a poker hall and something called C.S. Fly's, because evidently one entire fake Wild West town wasn't going to cut it without at least four distinct storefronts. The listing helpfully suggests this 4,400-square-foot fever dream "could be adapted" into more bedrooms, a basketball court or an indoor pool — as though the correct response to owning your own theme-park saloon is to knock it down and put in a Pilates studio.

And the numbers keep coming, almost as an afterthought at this point: 8 bathrooms serving just 4 bedrooms, a ratio so lopsided it suggests either extreme hospitality or a deep personal distrust of overnight guests. A gym, a sauna, a steam room. A 35-space, temperature-controlled garage with 220-volt wiring, purpose-built bays for motorhomes and boats, and — sure, why not — a further 15,000 square feet of unfinished basement just sitting there, doing absolutely nothing, being bigger than the house most of us grew up in.

We have genuinely no idea what kind of person commissions a commercial-grade neoclassical mansion and then hires a Six Flags designer to build Tombstone into the side of it, but we would like to shake their hand, and possibly move into the saloon permanently.
