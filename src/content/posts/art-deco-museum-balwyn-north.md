Shut the front gate behind you on the way in, because 18 Cascade Street is one lawn ornament short of needing a ticket booth. This is Balwyn North's own slice of the Art Deco "Ocean Liner" style — all curved brick, porthole windows and a soaring central chimney standing in for a funnel — and it has been kept in such immaculate condition that walking through it feels less like an inspection and more like a museum visit. We'd happily pay the entry fee. We'd also happily hire the security guard, because something this well preserved deserves a proper velvet rope.

A quick, deeply unserious history lesson: Art Deco arrived in the 1920s and 30s as design's answer to "what if buildings looked like they were about to win a speed record," borrowing streamlined curves from ocean liners, aeroplanes and the occasional very confident toaster. Melbourne got swept up in it hard, and the Riverside Estate clearly got the memo early, because this house has been doing its best impression of the RMS Queen Mary since long before anyone here was born. The curved walls, the banded brickwork, the nautical detailing — none of it is subtle, and none of it is meant to be.

Inside, the formal rooms play it relatively straight: a baby grand piano under a tiered crystal chandelier, parquetry flooring, decorative cornices doing laps around every ceiling, and enough traditional joinery to make a heritage architect weep with joy. It's genuinely grand, genuinely old-money, and genuinely still fully intact, which after ninety-odd years is no small achievement.

Then you go down the steps into the rumpus room, and the house reveals its actual personality. There is a full built-in timber bar, complete with glass shelving for the good bottles, sitting directly beside cane armchairs on a shagpile rug that has clearly hosted more than a few long evenings. Beyond it, under gold reed-print wallpaper, sits a full-size billiards table, racked and waiting. We are formally lodging a complaint with modern architecture on this point: conversation pits and proper wet bars have all but disappeared from new builds, and this house is Exhibit A for why that's a mistake. If you're designing anything from here on, put the bar back in.

The bedrooms carry on the theme upstairs, several with their own study nooks and two opening onto private balconies with genuinely panoramic views over the neighbourhood this place has been quietly outclassing for nearly a century. Gold fern wallpaper recurs like a signature, paired with built-in wardrobes and dressing tables that were clearly designed around these exact rooms rather than bought separately and squeezed in. The bathrooms split the difference between original powder-pink tiling and a fully marbled ensuite with gold tapware that looks like it was lifted straight off the ship this whole house is impersonating.

Six bedrooms, four bathrooms, two single garages, a home office, a workshop and a rear garden with its own sunroom addition and a magnolia currently putting on a full show — the scale here is genuinely substantial, and the estate agent's pitch to "large families, Art Deco devotees and visionary renovators" tracks. Whoever buys this either restores it lovingly or renovates it into something contemporary and, frankly, forgettable. We know which outcome we're rooting for.

Ninety years in, this house has survived every renovation trend going, kept its bar, kept its billiards table, and kept every last curve of its original silhouette. It deserves better than a quiet sale. It deserves a plaque.
