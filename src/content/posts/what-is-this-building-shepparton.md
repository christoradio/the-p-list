Nobody at 158 Nixon Street ever got the memo that houses in Shepparton are supposed to look like houses. Somewhere in the 1980s, somebody stood on this corner block, looked at the intersection of Nixon and Skene Streets, and decided the only appropriate response was a five-arch colonnaded portico flanked by two full towers of decorative brick lattice, the whole thing capped with a balustraded terrace that would not look out of place hosting a coronation. It has since spent decades being mistaken, by us and presumably half the town, for a church, a TAFE campus, or the regional headquarters of an organisation nobody's allowed to ask questions about.

The white columns alone are doing more architectural work than most entire streetscapes. Round the side, the theme keeps going with zero signs of fatigue — a second brick lattice tower, a matching red trim roofline, and enough terrazzo underfoot at the entrance to survive a small earthquake. Whoever built this had access to a very specific and very committed vision, and the confidence to apply it to all four sides of the building rather than just the bit visible from the street. That's the detail that actually earns this one a spot on the list: the commitment doesn't flag once you round the corner, which is more than we can say for most "statement" architecture.

Inside, the statement continues without so much as a breath. The entry hall runs a full spiral staircase, carpeted timber treads and all, with a grandfather clock wedged into the space underneath it because apparently a plain hallway closet wasn't going to say enough. The kitchen has kept its floor-to-ceiling timber cabinetry fully intact, alongside a framed print of the Last Supper built into its own custom alcove — positioned, we assume, to keep an eye on proceedings. A few cupboards along sits a stainless steel double fridge that looks like it was delivered from several decades in the future and simply never left.

The formal dining room seats ten comfortably under a full chandelier, in front of a stone feature fireplace and a fully stocked home bar that has clearly hosted its share of long lunches. A second living room carries chesterfield lounges and floor-to-ceiling drapery, with patterned carpet running wall to wall doing double duty as flooring and, in low light, as wallpaper. None of it reads as neglected — this is a house that has been lived in, cared for, and left almost entirely alone by trend for forty-odd years, which is exactly why it's here.

Held by the same family since it was built, this is the first time the property has been offered for sale, and the listing leans hard into that rarity. It's zoned Activity Centre, which in practical terms means live upstairs and run a business downstairs, lease it out as offices, or simply enjoy having five bedrooms and three bathrooms spread across two levels with room to spare. Parking runs to four cars split across two separate street frontages — a double garage off Nixon Street, a double carport off Skene — which tells you this was always meant to be a corner statement piece, not a quiet infill block that happened to get big ideas.

We don't know who signed off on the original brief here, and we suspect even they might struggle to explain it fully today. What we do know is that somewhere in Shepparton, a house has been quietly out-arching every church within a five kilometre radius for four decades, and it's finally letting someone else take a turn living in it.
